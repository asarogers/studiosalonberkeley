Retwist frequency is one of the first questions I get in my chair at Studio Salon. Someone sits down, pulls out their phone, and shows me screenshots of advice from three different loc accounts saying three different things. Two weeks. Four weeks. Eight weeks. Only when it gets messy. I've been a loctician in Berkeley long enough to know the honest answer sits somewhere in the middle, and it depends almost entirely on your hair, your lifestyle, and the condition of your roots.

## The 4-to-8 Week Rule (And Why It Isn't One Size Fits All)

The standard answer I give is four to eight weeks, and for most people, that's right. If you have fine, loose-curl hair and your new growth comes in fast, you might sit closer to the four-week mark. If you have dense, tightly coiled hair that locs down easily without much slippage, six to eight weeks is usually your sweet spot. What I push back on hard is the idea that you should be in my chair every two weeks. That's a schedule sold by stylists who want a full book, not one that protects your hair.

It also runs counter to what a retwist is actually for: giving new growth a chance to settle into the existing loc pattern without constant manipulation. Too frequent, and you never let the hair bond. Too infrequent, and the base of the loc starts to blend into a loose, fuzzy halo that's hard to tidy cleanly. Four to eight weeks gives us both — real growth to work with, and a tight, clean result at the scalp.

## What Over-Retwisting Actually Does to Your Roots

Retwisting is mechanical stress on the weakest part of your loc, the new growth at the scalp. Every time we twist, we put tension on hair that's often only a few millimeters long and just starting to pattern. Do that every two weeks, and two things happen.

First, the root gets thin. You'll see the base of the loc taper down to a pencil point, and eventually that loc breaks off at the scalp. I've re-attached more broken locs than I can count, and almost always on clients who came from salons with a two-week retwist habit. Second, the scalp itself gets irritated. Chronic tension pulls on follicles, and over time that shows up as traction alopecia around the hairline and part lines, especially at the edges, where the hair is finest. Once that damage is there, it's slow and frustrating to reverse.

Spacing retwists out isn't laziness. It's the single biggest thing you can do to keep your locs long, thick, and attached to your head.

## Signs Your Locs Are Asking for a Professional Touch-Up

Some people track it by the calendar. I prefer to read the hair. A few cues I look for: your new growth has a visible pattern break where the loose hair meets the mature loc, meaning the two textures aren't blending anymore. Your parts have drifted, and the grid you started with is starting to merge — two locs are marrying at the base, or one is splitting into a Y. You're seeing a halo of frizz an inch or more off the scalp that doesn't lay down even with product and a satin bonnet. Your locs feel loose at the root when you tug gently.

Any of those are signals to book. On the other hand, a little surface fuzz between retwists is normal and actually good. It means your locs are breathing and not being over-manipulated. If you live in Berkeley or anywhere in the East Bay with fog, humidity, and salt air off the Bay, you'll get a bit more frizz than someone in a drier climate, and that's fine. The goal is healthy locs, not plastic-perfect ones.

## Water-Only, Gel, or Butter Retwists

Not every retwist needs product. A water-only retwist is exactly what it sounds like — we mist the roots, twist, and let the hair's own texture do the work. It's my go-to for clients with tightly coiled hair that grips itself, and it leaves zero residue. The trade-off: it doesn't hold as long, maybe two to three weeks of pristine look before the roots start to puff.

Gel retwists use a loc-specific gel, not eco-style, which flakes and builds up, and give you a crisper, longer-lasting hold — three to five weeks before serious fuzz. The trade-off there is buildup if we overdo it, so I use a light hand and always follow with a clarifying wash next visit.

Butter retwists use a shea or mango butter instead of gel. They give shine and moisture, with less hold than gel, and are a great fit for dry scalps and colder months. At Studio Salon I ask every client which finish they want and why, because the right product depends on your hair, not on what's trending on loc-tok this month.

## Interlocking vs. Palm-Rolling: Which Is Right for You?

Palm-rolling is the technique most people picture. We separate the new growth, apply product if we're using any, and roll the root between our palms to train it into the loc below. It's gentle, it gives locs a classic rope-like shape, and it's the method I prefer for mature locs that have a clear pattern.

Interlocking is different. We use a tool, or our fingers, to pass the loc through its own base in a set rotation, literally knitting the new growth into the existing loc. Interlocking lasts much longer between retwists because the root is mechanically locked, not just rolled. That makes it popular with swimmers, athletes, and anyone who washes frequently.

The trade-off: done too often or too tightly, interlocking can weaken the root and create thin spots. I interlock for specific clients — people whose palm-rolled retwists never hold, or those who want six-plus weeks of clean roots. Neither method is better; they're tools. A good loctician picks the right one for your hair, your schedule, and your goals, and isn't afraid to switch between them over the life of your locs.

## Home Care That Extends the Time Between Salon Visits

You can stretch a clean retwist noticeably with a few habits.

### Five home-care moves that actually work

1. Sleep in a satin bonnet or on a satin pillowcase every night, no exceptions. Cotton shreds frizz into your new growth faster than almost anything else.
2. Wash every two to four weeks with a residue-free shampoo, and don't skip it. Clean locs hold their shape better than dirty ones.
3. Mist the roots with water and a light oil like jojoba or grapeseed every few days to keep them supple. Dry hair frizzes; moisturized hair lays down.
4. Re-palm-roll individual locs between salon visits if a few are misbehaving — just those, not the whole head.
5. Avoid heavy creams, butters marketed for loose natural hair, and anything with wax. They build up and cut retwist life in half.

Do those consistently, and you'll comfortably push to six or eight weeks between appointments, with locs that still look fresh when you walk in.
